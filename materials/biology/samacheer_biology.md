# Unit 1: The Living World and Animal Kingdom (உயிருலகம் மற்றும் விலங்குலகம்)

<!-- page: 1 -->

Diversity of the Living World and Taxonomy:
Biology is the science of life and living organisms. Living organisms exhibit distinct characteristics: growth, reproduction, metabolism, cellular organization, and consciousness or response to stimuli.
Taxonomy (வகைப்பாட்டியல்) is the branch of science dealing with the identification, nomenclature, and classification of organisms.
Systematics encompasses taxonomy along with evolutionary relationships (phylogeny) among organisms.
Carolus Linnaeus is considered the Father of Modern Taxonomy. He established the Binomial Nomenclature system where every organism is given a scientific name comprising two words: the Genus name (capitalized) and the Species epithet (lowercase), both printed in italics or underlined when handwritten (e.g., *Homo sapiens*, *Panthera leo*).
Rules of nomenclature are governed by the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature (ICZN) and the International Code of Botanical Nomenclature (ICBN).
The taxonomic hierarchy from lowest to highest category is:
Species -> Genus -> Family -> Order -> Class -> Phylum (or Division) -> Kingdom.

Levels of Animal Organization and Body Symmetry:
Animals are multicellular, heterotrophic eukaryotes belonging to Kingdom Animalia.
1. Levels of Organization:
- Cellular level: Cells are arranged as loose cell aggregates without true tissues (Phylum Porifera - sponges).
- Tissue level: Cells performing the same function are organized into distinct tissues (Phylum Cnidaria and Ctenophora).
- Organ level: Tissues group together to form specialized functional organs (Phylum Platyhelminthes).
- Organ system level: Organs associate into integrated organ systems (Annelids, Arthropods, Molluscs, Echinoderms, and Chordates).

2. Body Symmetry:
- Asymmetrical: Body cannot be divided into two equal halves along any plane passing through the center (most sponges).
- Radial Symmetry: Any plane passing through the central oral-aboral axis divides the body into identical mirror-image halves (Cnidarians, Ctenophores, and adult Echinoderms).
- Bilateral Symmetry: Only one single sagittal plane divides the animal into identical right and left halves (Platyhelminthes to Chordates).

3. Germ Layers:
- Diploblastic animals: Cells are arranged in two embryonic germ layers - outer ectoderm and inner endoderm, separated by an undifferentiated non-cellular gelatinous mesoglea (Cnidarians).
- Triploblastic animals: Developing embryo possesses three primary germ layers - outer ectoderm, middle mesoderm, and inner endoderm (Platyhelminthes to Chordates).

Coelom (Body Cavity) Classification:
The coelom (உடற்குழி) is the fluid-filled body cavity lined on all sides by mesoderm, situated between the alimentary canal and the body wall.
1. Acoelomates: Lack a body cavity. The space between body wall and gut is filled with mesodermal parenchyma tissue (Phylum Platyhelminthes - flatworms).
2. Pseudocoelomates: Body cavity is not lined by mesoderm; instead, mesoderm is scattered in pouches between ectoderm and endoderm (Phylum Aschelminthes / Nematoda - roundworms).
3. Eucoelomates (True Coelomates): Body cavity is completely lined by mesodermal peritoneum.
- Schizocoelomates: Coelom formed by the splitting of embryonic mesodermal bands (Annelida, Arthropoda, Mollusca).
- Enterocoelomates: Coelom formed by the outpouching of the embryonic archenteron (Echinodermata, Hemichordata, Chordata).

Survey of Invertebrate Phyla:
1. Phylum Porifera (Sponges):
Pore-bearing, mostly marine, sessile animals. Water enters through microscopic pores called ostia into a central cavity called spongocoel and exits through the osculum. Flagellated collar cells called choanocytes line the spongocoel and canals, maintaining water current and capturing food. Skeleton consists of calcareous or siliceous spicules or spongin fibres. Hermaphrodite; reproduce asexually by fragmentation or gemmules, and sexually by gametes. Examples: *Sycon*, *Spongilla* (freshwater sponge), *Euspongia* (bath sponge).

2. Phylum Cnidaria (Coelenterata):
Aquatic, radially symmetrical diploblastic animals with a central gastrovascular cavity (coelenteron) and single opening (hypostome). Characterized by specialized stinging cells called cnidocytes (or nematocysts) used for anchorage, defense, and capturing prey. Exhibit two body forms: sessile, cylindrical Polyp (e.g., *Hydra*, *Adamsia*) and umbrella-shaped, free-swimming Medusa (e.g., *Aurelia* - jellyfish). Many cnidarians exhibit alternation of generations (metagenesis) where polyps reproduce asexually to form medusae, and medusae reproduce sexually to form polyps (e.g., *Obelia*).

3. Phylum Platyhelminthes (Flatworms):
Dorso-ventrally flattened, triploblastic, acoelomate animals. Parasitic forms possess hooks and suckers for attachment to the host. Specialized excretory and osmoregulatory cells called flame cells (solenocytes) are present. Examples: *Taenia solium* (tapeworm), *Fasciola hepatica* (liver fluke), *Planaria* (exhibits high regeneration capacity).

4. Phylum Aschelminthes (Roundworms / Nematodes):
Cylindrical, pseudocoelomate worms with a complete alimentary canal having a muscular pharynx. Sexes are separate (dioecious), often showing sexual dimorphism (females are longer than males). Examples: *Ascaris lumbricoides* (intestinal roundworm), *Wuchereria bancrofti* (filarial worm causing elephantiasis), *Ancylostoma* (hookworm).

Higher Invertebrates and Chordates:
5. Phylum Annelida (Segmented Worms):
Characterized by metameric segmentation (metamerism). Possess longitudinal and circular muscles for locomotion. Locomotory structures include chitinous setae (in earthworms) or lateral appendages called parapodia (in *Nereis*). Excretion and osmoregulation by tubular nephridia. Possess a closed circulatory system with hemoglobin dissolved in plasma. Examples: *Pheretima* / *Megascolex* (earthworm), *Hirudinaria* (blood-sucking leech).

6. Phylum Arthropoda (Jointed Appendages):
Largest phylum of Kingdom Animalia, comprising over 80% of all described animal species. Body divided into head, thorax, and abdomen (or cephalothorax and abdomen), covered by a tough chitinous exoskeleton that is periodically shed through ecdysis (moulting). Open circulatory system with hemolymph. Respiratory organs: gills (prawns), book gills (horseshoe crab *Limulus*), book lungs (scorpions, spiders), or tracheal system (insects). Excretion by Malpighian tubules, green glands (antennal glands in crustaceans), or coxal glands. Examples: *Periplaneta americana* (cockroach), *Apis* (honeybee), *Bombyx* (silkworm).

7. Phylum Mollusca (Soft-Bodied Animals):
Second largest animal phylum. Body is unsegmented, covered by a calcareous shell, and differentiated into head, muscular foot (for locomotion), and visceral hump. The soft, spongy layer of skin over the visceral hump forms the mantle. The mantle cavity contains feather-like gills (ctenidia) for respiration and excretion. The mouth contains a rasping, file-like feeding organ with transverse rows of chitinous teeth called the radula. Examples: *Pila* (apple snail), *Pinctada* (pearl oyster), *Octopus* (devil fish).

8. Phylum Echinodermata (Spiny-Skinned Animals):
Exclusively marine animals. Adults exhibit pentamerous radial symmetry, whereas larvae are bilaterally symmetrical. Possess an endoskeleton of calcareous ossicles. Characteristic water vascular system (ambulacral system) connected to tube feet (podia) utilized for locomotion, food capture, and respiration. Examples: *Asterias* (starfish), *Echinus* (sea urchin), *Cucumaria* (sea cucumber).

9. Phylum Chordata:
Characterized by four fundamental diagnostic features:
- Presence of a dorsal, solid notochord at some stage of life.
- A dorsal hollow tubular nerve cord.
- Paired pharyngeal gill slits.
- Post-anal tail.
Subphyla: Urochordata (notochord present only in larval tail, e.g., *Ascidia*), Cephalochordata (notochord extends from head to tail throughout life, e.g., *Branchiostoma* / *Amphioxus*), and Vertebrata (notochord is replaced by a cartilaginous or bony vertebral column in adults).


# Unit 2: Digestion and Absorption (செரித்தல் மற்றும் உட்கிரகித்தல்)

<!-- page: 6 -->

Anatomy of Human Digestive System:
Digestion is the mechanical and biochemical conversion of complex, non-diffusible food molecules into simple, absorbable forms.
The human alimentary canal begins with the mouth and buccal cavity.
Human dentition is characterized by:
- Thecodont: Each tooth is embedded in a deep socket of the jaw bone.
- Diphyodont: Two sets of teeth during lifetime - deciduous/milk teeth replaced by permanent adult teeth.
- Heterodont: Four distinct types of teeth - Incisors (I) for cutting, Canines (C) for tearing, Premolars (PM) and Molars (M) for grinding and crushing.
The human adult dental formula is:
2123 / 2123 (Incisors: 2/2, Canines: 1/1, Premolars: 2/2, Molars: 3/3 = 16 per jaw, total 32 teeth).
Enamel is the hardest biological substance in the human body, composed of calcium phosphate (hydroxyapatite), secreted by ameloblasts.

The stomach is a J-shaped muscular organ divided into four regions:
1. Cardiac region (near esophageal entrance guarded by the gastroesophageal sphincter).
2. Fundus (dome-shaped upper portion filled with air/gas).
3. Body (main central portion).
4. Pyloric region (narrow distal portion opening into duodenum through pyloric sphincter).

<!-- page: 7 -->

Digestive Secretions and Enzymatic Digestion:
1. Saliva:
Secreted by three pairs of salivary glands: Parotid (near cheek, largest), Submandibular/Submaxillary (lower jaw angle), and Sublingual (beneath tongue).
Daily secretion: 1 to 1.5 litres; pH = 6.8.
Contains Salivary Amylase (Ptyalin), which hydrolyzes approximately 30% of dietary starch into maltose (a disaccharide), and lysozyme (antibacterial agent).

2. Gastric Juice:
Secreted by gastric mucosal glands (pH 1.5 to 2.5):
- Chief cells (Peptic or Zymogenic cells): Secrete inactive proenzymes Pepsinogen and Prorennin.
- Parietal or Oxyntic cells: Secrete Hydrochloric Acid (HCl, provides optimal acidic pH and converts pepsinogen into active pepsin) and Castle's Intrinsic Factor (essential for vitamin B12 absorption in the ileum; deficiency causes pernicious anaemia).
- Mucous neck cells: Secrete mucus and bicarbonates to lubricate and protect gastric mucosa from excoriation by concentrated HCl.
Pepsin hydrolyzes proteins into proteoses and peptones.

3. Pancreatic Juice and Bile:
- Liver: Largest internal organ (~1.5 kg). Hepatocytes secrete Bile (pH 7.8 - 8.6), stored in the gallbladder. Bile contains no digestive enzymes; it contains bile salts (Sodium glycocholate and taurocholate) that emulsify fats into tiny droplets (micelles) and bile pigments (bilirubin and biliverdin).
- Pancreatic juice: Alkaline secretion (pH 8.4) rich in proenzymes: Trypsinogen (activated by intestinal enterokinase into active trypsin), Chymotrypsinogen, Procarboxypeptidase, Pancreatic Amylase, Pancreatic Lipase (steapsin), and Nucleases.
- Small Intestine: Complete digestion occurs in the duodenum and jejunum. Intestinal juice (Succus Entericus, pH 7.8) contains maltase, lactase, sucrase, dipeptidases, and intestinal lipases.

Nutritional Disorders:
Protein-Energy Malnutrition (PEM):
- Kwashiorkor: Caused by severe dietary protein deficiency unaccompanied by calorie deficiency, typically in children aged 1 to 5 years. Symptoms: extensive edema of limbs and face (pot belly), stunted growth, pigmentation of skin, thinning of limbs.
- Marasmus: Caused by simultaneous deficiency of both proteins and calories in infants under one year. Symptoms: extreme emaciation, dry and wrinkled skin, absence of subcutaneous fat, prominent ribs, without edema.


# Unit 3: Breathing and Exchange of Gases (சுவாசம் மற்றும் வாயுப் பரிமாற்றம்)

<!-- page: 8 -->

Respiratory Organs and Mechanism of Breathing:
In humans, atmospheric air enters via external nostrils -> nasal cavity -> pharynx -> glottis of larynx (voice box protected by cartilaginous flap epiglottis) -> trachea -> primary, secondary, and tertiary bronchi -> terminal bronchioles -> respiratory alveoli.
Alveoli are thin-walled, vascularized bag-like structures providing ~70 to 100 m^2 surface area for gas exchange.
The respiratory membrane consists of three layers: alveolar squamous epithelium, capillary endothelium, and intervening thin basement membrane, with a total thickness under 0.5 micrometres.

Mechanism of Pulmonary Ventilation:
1. Inspiration (Active process):
Contraction of the dome-shaped diaphragm increases thoracic volume in the antero-posterior axis. Contraction of External Intercostal Muscles lifts ribs and sternum, increasing thoracic volume in the dorso-ventral axis.
Intra-pulmonary pressure drops below atmospheric pressure (-1 mm Hg), and air rushes into the lungs.
2. Expiration (Passive process during quiet breathing):
Relaxation of diaphragm and external intercostal muscles restores thoracic volume, intra-pulmonary pressure rises above atmospheric (+1 mm Hg), expelling air.

Respiratory Volumes and Capacities:
- Tidal Volume (TV): Volume of air inspired or expired during normal quiet breathing (~500 mL).
- Inspiratory Reserve Volume (IRV): Extra volume inspired forcefully above tidal volume (2500 - 3000 mL).
- Expiratory Reserve Volume (ERV): Extra volume expired forcefully after normal tidal expiration (1000 - 1100 mL).
- Residual Volume (RV): Volume remaining in lungs even after maximal forced expiration (1100 - 1200 mL). RV cannot be measured by a spirometer.
- Vital Capacity (VC): Maximum volume exhaled after maximal forced inspiration:
VC = TV + IRV + ERV (~4600 mL).
- Total Lung Capacity (TLC): Total volume of air accommodated in lungs after forced inspiration:
TLC = VC + RV = TV + IRV + ERV + RV (~5800 mL).

Gas Transport and Respiratory Regulation:
1. Oxygen Transport:
- 3% dissolved in blood plasma.
- 97% transported bound to hemoglobin as Oxyhaemoglobin (Hb4O8). Each hemoglobin molecule contains four heme iron (Fe2+) groups and can carry up to 4 molecules of O2.
Oxygen-Haemoglobin Dissociation Curve:
A sigmoid (S-shaped) curve relating percentage saturation of hemoglobin to partial pressure of oxygen (pO2).
Bohr Effect: Decreased pH (increased H+ concentration), increased pCO2, or increased temperature shifts the curve to the RIGHT, lowering hemoglobin affinity for O2 and promoting oxygen unloading at metabolically active tissues.

2. Carbon Dioxide Transport:
- 7% dissolved in plasma.
- 23% bound to globin amino groups of hemoglobin as Carbamino-haemoglobin.
- 70% transported as Bicarbonate ions (HCO3-).
In RBCs, the zinc-containing enzyme Carbonic Anhydrase rapidly catalyzes:
CO2 + H2O <-> H2CO3 <-> H+ + HCO3-
Chloride Shift (Hamburger Phenomenon):
As HCO3- ions diffuse out of erythrocytes into plasma down concentration gradient, chloride ions (Cl-) diffuse from plasma into erythrocytes to maintain electrical neutrality.

Respiratory Disorders:
- Asthma: Allergic inflammation of bronchi and bronchioles causing wheezing, coughing, and difficulty breathing.
- Emphysema: Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) characterized by destruction and breakdown of alveolar walls, drastically reducing gas exchange surface area, primarily caused by cigarette smoking.


# Unit 4: Body Fluids and Circulation (சுற்றோட்டம் மற்றும் உடல் திரவங்கள்)

<!-- page: 10 -->

Blood Composition and Coagulation:
Blood is a specialized fluid connective tissue consisting of plasma (55%) and cellular formed elements (45%).
1. Plasma: Straw-colored viscous fluid (90-92% water, 6-8% proteins: Albumin maintains colloid osmotic pressure; Globulins provide antibodies/immunity; Fibrinogen is essential for blood clotting).
2. Formed Elements:
- Erythrocytes (RBCs): Biconcave, enucleated discs in adult mammals (5 - 5.5 million/mm^3). Contain hemoglobin (12-16 g/100 mL). Lifespan = 120 days; destroyed in spleen ("graveyard of RBCs").
- Leucocytes (WBCs): Nucleated, defensive cells (6000 - 8000/mm^3). Granulocytes: Neutrophils (60-65%, phagocytic), Eosinophils (2-3%, allergic responses), Basophils (0.5-1%, secrete histamine, serotonin, heparin). Agranulocytes: Lymphocytes (20-25%, B and T cells for humoral and cell-mediated immunity), Monocytes (6-8%, phagocytic macrophages).
- Thrombocytes (Platelets): Non-nucleated fragments produced from megakaryocytes in bone marrow (150,000 - 350,000/mm^3). Release thromboplastin for clotting.

ABO Blood Groups and Rh Incompatibility:
Karl Landsteiner discovered the ABO system determined by antigens A and B on RBC surface:
- Group A: Antigen A, antibody anti-B.
- Group B: Antigen B, antibody anti-A.
- Group AB: Antigens A and B, no antibodies (Universal Recipient).
- Group O: No antigens, antibodies anti-A and anti-B (Universal Donor).
Erythroblastosis Foetalis (Haemolytic disease of the newborn): Occurs when an Rh-negative mother carries an Rh-positive fetus. In subsequent pregnancies with an Rh+ fetus, maternal anti-Rh IgG antibodies cross the placenta and destroy fetal red blood cells, causing severe hemolytic anemia and jaundice. Prevented by administering anti-Rh antibodies (Rhogam) to the mother immediately after delivery.

Cardiac Cycle, Conduction, and ECG:
The human heart is myogenic, four-chambered, enclosed within double-walled pericardium.
Origin and Conduction of Cardiac Impulse:
1. Sinoatrial Node (SAN): Located in right upper wall of right atrium. Spontaneously generates rhythmic action potentials at 70-75 pulses/min. Hence named the Pacemaker of the heart.
2. Atrioventricular Node (AVN): In lower left wall of right atrium near inter-atrial septum.
3. Bundle of His (AV Bundle): Runs down the interventricular septum, dividing into right and left bundle branches.
4. Purkinje Fibres: Spread through ventricular walls, causing rapid synchronized ventricular contraction.

Cardiac Output:
Stroke Volume (SV) is the volume of blood pumped by each ventricle per contraction (~70 mL).
Cardiac Output (CO) = Stroke Volume * Heart Rate = 70 mL * 72 beats/min = approximately 5040 mL/min (~5 L/min).

Electrocardiogram (ECG):
Graphical recording of electrical voltage variations generated by the heart during each cardiac cycle:
- P Wave: Depolarization (excitation) of both atria leading to atrial contraction.
- QRS Complex: Depolarization of ventricles initiating ventricular systole.
- T Wave: Repolarization of ventricles (return from excited to relaxed resting state).

<!-- page: 12 -->

Modes of Nitrogenous Excretion:
1. Ammonotelism: Excretion of toxic ammonia (NH3) requiring vast amounts of water (300-500 mL water per gram of ammonia). Common in aquatic bony fishes, aquatic amphibians, and aquatic insects.
2. Ureotelism: Excretion of less toxic urea. Ammonia is synthesized into urea in liver hepatocytes via the Krebs-Henseleit Ornithine Cycle. Common in terrestrial mammals, adult amphibians, and cartilaginous fishes (sharks).
3. Uricotelism: Excretion of least toxic, insoluble uric acid as a semi-solid paste or pellet, requiring minimal water (10 mL per gram). Adaptation for water conservation in reptiles, birds, land snails, and terrestrial insects.

Structure and Function of the Nephron:
The functional unit of the human kidney is the nephron (~1 million per kidney).
Consists of:
1. Malpighian Body (Renal Corpuscle):
- Glomerulus: Tuft of capillaries fed by afferent arteriole and drained by narrower efferent arteriole.
- Bowman's Capsule: Double-walled cup with specialized podocyte epithelial cells possessing filtration slits.
2. Renal Tubule:
- Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT): Simple cuboidal brush-border epithelium. Reabsorbs ~70-80% of electrolytes and water, and 100% of glucose and amino acids by active transport.
- Loop of Henle: Hairpin loop with thin descending limb (permeable to water, impermeable to electrolytes) and thick ascending limb (impermeable to water, actively transports electrolytes).
- Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT): Conditional reabsorption of Na+ and water under aldosterone and ADH control.
- Collecting Duct: Extends from cortex into deep medulla, reabsorbing water to produce concentrated urine.

Urine Formation Mechanisms:
1. Glomerular Ultrafiltration: Driven by net filtration pressure (NFP ~10 mm Hg). Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) is 125 mL/min (180 litres/day).
2. Tubular Reabsorption: 99% of filtrate is reabsorbed; only ~1.5 litres of urine is excreted per day.
3. Tubular Secretion: Active secretion of H+, K+, and ammonia into tubular filtrate maintaining acid-base balance and blood pH (7.4).

Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS):
When GFR, blood volume, or blood pressure drops, the Juxtaglomerular Cells (JG cells) of the kidney release Renin into blood.
Renin converts plasma angiotensinogen into Angiotensin I.
Angiotensin Converting Enzyme (ACE) in lung endothelium converts Angiotensin I into Angiotensin II (a potent vasoconstrictor that raises blood pressure).
Angiotensin II stimulates the adrenal cortex to secrete Aldosterone, which enhances Na+ and water reabsorption at the DCT, restoring blood volume, blood pressure, and GFR.


# Unit 6: Neural Control and Chemical Coordination (நரம்பு மற்றும் நாளமில்லாச் சுரப்பிகள்)

<!-- page: 13 -->

Nerve Impulse Generation and Synaptic Transmission:
Neurons are structural and functional units of neural tissue, consisting of a cell body (cyton/soma), branching dendrites, and an elongated axon terminating in synaptic knobs.
1. Resting Membrane Potential (-70 mV):
The axonal membrane is more permeable to K+ and nearly impermeable to Na+.
Active Sodium-Potassium Pump (Na+/K+ ATPase) expels 3 Na+ ions out of the cell for every 2 K+ ions pumped into the axoplasm, utilizing 1 ATP molecule. This establishes an electro-negative interior relative to the exterior.
2. Action Potential (Depolarization, +30 mV):
When a threshold stimulus arrives, voltage-gated Na+ channels snap open, and rapid influx of Na+ reverses polarity from -70 mV to +30 mV (depolarization).
3. Repolarization:
Na+ channels close, voltage-gated K+ channels open, and rapid efflux of K+ restores resting negative potential.

Chemical Synapse Transmission:
When an action potential reaches the axon terminal, voltage-gated Ca2+ channels open, allowing Ca2+ influx.
Synaptic vesicles fuse with presynaptic membrane, releasing neurotransmitter (e.g., Acetylcholine) by exocytosis into the synaptic cleft (~20 nm).
Neurotransmitter binds to specific ligand-gated receptor channels on the postsynaptic membrane, generating excitatory postsynaptic potentials (EPSP).

<!-- page: 14 -->

Endocrine Glands and Hormone Actions:
Hormones are non-nutrient intercellular chemical messengers produced in trace amounts, secreted directly into blood by ductless endocrine glands.
1. Hypothalamus and Pituitary Gland (Hypophysis):
- Anterior Pituitary (Adenohypophysis): Secretes Growth Hormone (GH - hypersecretion causes Gigantism in children and Acromegaly in adults; hyposecretion causes Pituitary Dwarfism), Thyroid Stimulating Hormone (TSH), Adrenocorticotropic Hormone (ACTH), Follicle Stimulating Hormone (FSH), and Luteinizing Hormone (LH).
- Posterior Pituitary (Neurohypophysis): Stores and releases Oxytocin (induces vigorous uterine contractions during parturition and milk ejection) and Vasopressin / Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH - promotes water reabsorption at collecting ducts; hyposecretion causes Diabetes Insipidus).

2. Thyroid Gland:
Follicular cells secrete Thyroxine (T4) and Triiodothyronine (T3), requiring iodine.
- Hypothyroidism: Causes Simple Goitre (enlarged thyroid), Cretinism (mental retardation and stunted growth in children), and Myxoedema (lethargy and puffiness in adults).
- Hyperthyroidism: Exophthalmic Goitre or Graves' Disease (elevated BMR, protruding eyeballs, weight loss).
- Parafollicular cells secrete Calcitonin (hypocalcemic hormone, lowers blood calcium level).

3. Parathyroid Glands:
Secrete Parathyroid Hormone (PTH / Collip's hormone), a hypercalcemic hormone that raises blood Ca2+ levels by stimulating bone resorption (osteoclasts) and renal Ca2+ reabsorption. Antagonist to calcitonin.

4. Endocrine Pancreas (Islets of Langerhans):
- Alpha cells: Secrete Glucagon (hyperglycemic hormone, stimulates glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis in liver).
- Beta cells: Secrete Insulin (hypoglycemic hormone, stimulates glucose uptake and glycogenesis in muscle and adipose tissue).
Hyposecretion or insulin resistance causes Diabetes Mellitus (hyperglycemia, glycosuria, polyuria, polydipsia, ketonuria).
